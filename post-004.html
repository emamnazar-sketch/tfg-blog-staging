Last night a mom wrote into r/Parenting with a question I could not stop thinking about. Her tween daughter is fine with her friends. Happy, loud, herself. But around bigger, louder personalities, she goes quiet. She shrinks a little. And her mom asked: how do I build her confidence?

I am a dad of six, so I have watched this exact thing happen more than once. Here is the line I wrote back to her: feeling safe at home made my kids braver outside.

But first, the numbers. Because this mom is not alone, not even close.

The numbers on tween confidence

A survey from September this year asked 5,000 girls across the UK how confident they feel taking risks. In lower secondary school, 67% said they feel confident. By the time they reach sixth form, that number has fallen to 48%. A second number in the same survey: 81% of 14-year-old girls believe they can overcome challenges. By 18, only 68% still believe it.

And parents are seeing it at home. A 2026 survey of 419 parents found that 78% of them have watched their child change what they do, wear, or choose because of what other people might think. Almost half — 46% — say their kids ask for reassurance or approval first, before they even try.

Read that again. Eight out of ten parents have watched their kid edit themselves for the room.

What the numbers mean

This is the part that changed how I see it. The researchers behind the 5,000-girl survey said confidence does not grow in a straight line as kids get older. It wobbles. Puberty, harder school, friendships getting complicated, and suddenly a girl is much more aware of failure, criticism, and judgment. Taking a risk — speaking up, being loud in a loud room — starts to feel expensive.

So when your tween goes quiet around big personalities, she is not broken. She is reading the room. She knows exactly how loud that room is. The quiet is her protection.

That reframe helped me stop trying to fix the quiet and start trying to build the trust underneath it.

Confidence is not loudness. It is trusting yourself when the room is loud.

Here is what I am trying

I am not an expert. I am a tired dad trying things and watching what happens. Three things I am trying with my own kids:

One-on-one time where I mostly listen. Not a lecture. Not a talk about confidence. Just time with one kid at a time, where they talk more than I do. My 13-year-old talks a lot more in the car, side by side, when I am not looking right at him. I learned to shut up and let him finish. That is the whole move.

Small real jobs, so they know they can do real things. Cooking a meal the family actually eats. Fixing something small that actually needs fixing. Helping a little brother with something real. Confidence does not come from me telling them they are great. It comes from them doing a hard thing and seeing it work.

Waiting when they go quiet in front of loud people. This is the hard one. When a bigger personality talks over my kid, my hand wants to jump in and rescue them. I am trying not to. I wait. I let them finish their own sentence, even if it takes a while, even if it is awkward. The message is: your words are worth waiting for. I want them to believe that before they walk into any loud room.

This is not a magic fix. Some days it works and some days my kid still goes quiet, and I sit there feeling like I failed. But here is what I am seeing over time: kids who feel safe being quiet at home get braver outside it. The safety comes first. The bravery follows.

If she is confident with her friends but quiet around big personalities, that is not a confidence problem. That is a kid who already has confidence — she just spends it where she feels safe.

Your job is not to make her loud. Your job is to make her safe enough to be heard.

Two small things to try this week

1. Give her one real job this week. Not a chore she already does. A job with a real result the family can see — cook one meal, fix one thing, plan one outing. Then say nothing about confidence. Just eat the meal and say thank you. Let the result do the talking.

2. Practice finishing sentences at home. At dinner, when a sibling talks over her, gently hold the floor: "Hold on, let her finish." Do it for every kid, every time, so it is a house rule and not a spotlight on her. She will start to believe her sentences are worth finishing. That belief travels with her into loud rooms.

And here is why this matters more than grades: in a 2026 survey, parents picked confidence (24%) as what they want most for their kids — nearly double the ones who picked academic achievement (13%). We all want this. We are all trying.

Questions parents ask

Should I tell my tween to just speak up more?

Telling a quiet kid to be louder usually backfires — it tells her the quiet is the problem. Try building the trust underneath instead: real jobs she can do, and a home where her sentences get finished. The speaking up comes on its own, later, in rooms where she feels safe.

What if she is confident with friends but not with adults or new kids?

That is actually good news. It means the confidence is already there — she just spends it where she feels safe. Your job is to make more rooms feel safe, starting with your house, and to give her small wins in slightly bigger rooms over time.

Is it normal for confidence to drop around age 12?

Yes. Research keeps finding the same pattern: girls' confidence to take risks drops sharply through the school years — one 2026 survey of 5,000 girls found it falling from 67% to 48%. Puberty, harder schoolwork, and more complicated friendships all land at once. The drop is normal; it is not permanent, and small steady experiences of doing hard things rebuild it.
