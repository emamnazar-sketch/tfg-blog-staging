How Do I Give My Quieter Child Enough Attention When Their Sibling Always Needs Something?

A mom asked the question out loud in a parenting group this week. She has one child who always needs something — help, attention, a problem solved right now. And a quieter child who does not ask for much. Her question was simple: "How do I give my quieter child enough attention when their sibling always needs something?"

If you have more than one kid, you already know this feeling. It sits in your chest at night.

The numbers say you are not the only one feeling it

In April 2026, a parenting write-up pulled together sociological studies on this exact thing. The headline number: around 70% of mothers report feeling closer to one child. Not a little. Seven out of ten.

The therapists in that write-up said something I cannot stop thinking about. It is not the big things that shape a child's sense of being favored. It is the small daily stuff — tone of voice, affection, how time gets handed out across the day. The little behaviors. That is what the kids are reading.

A second piece, from January 2026, pulled in sociologist J. Jill Suitor's study of more than 500 mothers. About two in three parents named a favorite child — and the pick usually stayed the same for decades. Big life events did not change it. Shared values did.

Only 10% of parents would admit a favorite publicly, according to a UK write-up this month. But the kids notice anyway. Here is the number that got me:

More than half the time, parents and children named different children as the favorite.

Read that again. We think we are hiding it. The kids are doing the math on their own — and they are not always doing it the way we think.

The turn: it is not the minutes

Here is where I stopped trying to fix the schedule and started fixing something else.

A peer-reviewed study published in Frontiers in Psychology this year found that fairness perception is the key. When a difference is about age or need — the baby needs more hands right now — children read it as a "reasonable difference," not an injustice. What they cannot read is a difference with no explanation.

So the quieter child is not asking you to split your day into equal slices. They are asking one question: am I seen?

And the answer they are listening for is not in your calendar. It is in your tone, your affection, your face when you turn toward them.

Me too — and I have the receipt

I am a dad of six. Some of my kids take up more room in the day than others. That is just true.

My 13-year-old told me once that I talk a lot and do not listen to him. He was right. That line sat in my chest for days. I wrote about that night and what we are trying now here — the learning part for me is closing my mouth and letting him finish. (I am still bad at this part.)

And this week I told a mom in a parenting group what I am already doing for my tween: one-on-one time with each of my kids, where I mostly listen. Not a big event. Just their time. It is the one practice I keep coming back to, because it is the only one that answers the quiet kid's real question.

Your quieter child is not asking for equal minutes. They are asking for one small proof that they are not invisible.

Here is the part I was missing

Your kids are not watching your calendar. They are watching your face.

The 2026 research keeps saying the same thing in different words: the child's perception is the whole game. The kids who feel seen hold up better. And what they see is tone, affection, time — the small daily behaviors, not the grand gestures.

Once I heard it that way, the job got smaller. I cannot give six kids equal minutes. I do not need to. I need each of them to feel chosen on purpose, at least once a day.

That is the line I am trying to walk now.

Here's what I'm trying

Three small things. All experiments. I do not know yet if they work.

1. One-on-one time where I mostly listen. Each kid gets their own window — not long, just theirs. And I try to do more listening than talking. This is the practice I already told a mom about this week, and it is the one I trust most. The quieter kid does not need a big event. They need one window that says this part of my day belongs to you.

2. Say the reason out loud. When one child gets more of me in a day, I am starting to name why: "She is little, and this part is about age." The 2026 study says kids read age-or-need differences as fair — when they can see the reason. I used to hope they would guess. I do not leave it to guessing anymore.

3. Track my small behaviors for one week. Tone. Affection. Time. Just a note at night: who did I sit with today, and how did I sound with each of them? The therapists in the 2026 write-up suggested rotating one-on-one activities and watching the patterns. I want to see my own pattern before I tell myself there is no pattern.

This is not a magic fix. One honest caveat: with six kids, I will still miss days, and some days one child will still need everything I have. The goal is not a perfect split. The goal is that no kid goes a whole day without feeling chosen.

Questions parents ask

How much one-on-one time does each child need?

There is no magic number in the research. What shows up again and again is not the length of the session but the small daily behaviors — tone, affection, time handed out across the day. A short window of fully undivided attention, where the child feels like the only person in the room, does more than a whole evening of distracted company. Start small and keep it steady.

Should I treat all my kids exactly the same?

No — and trying to can backfire. A peer-reviewed 2026 study found that children read differences based on age or need as a "reasonable difference," not unfairness. A 9-year-old and a 19-year-old do not need identical treatment; they need treatment that fits their age and is explained out loud. Fairness is about understanding, not matching.

What if one child truly needs more attention right now?

That is normal family life, not failure. The thing to protect is how the quieter child reads it. Say the reason out loud — "he needs more help with this right now, and that is okay" — and watch your tone and affection with each of them in the small moments. Kids forgive the unequal minutes when the reason makes sense and they still feel seen.
