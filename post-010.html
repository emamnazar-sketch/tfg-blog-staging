A parent wrote this week that she is at her wit's end. Her daughter is in her last year of high school. She will not get up for school in the morning.

The parent says she has tried everything. She has even worked with the school. The mornings still go the same way.

If that is your house, I want to start with the part that helped me breathe a little: your teen is not the only one. This is not a small problem in a few homes. The numbers say it is most homes.

The numbers: most teens start school short on sleep

The CDC just released its newest youth survey, with data from 2025. Only about 1 in 4 high school students — 24% — got at least 8 hours of sleep on an average school night. That number barely moved from 2023. The CDC also says short sleep is linked with poor mental health and trouble paying attention.

Now hold that next to what teens actually need. Sleep doctors at the American Academy of Sleep Medicine say teenagers ages 13 to 18 should sleep 8 to 10 hours a night to be healthy.

So most teens need 8 to 10 hours, and about 3 in 4 are not even reaching 8 on a school night. The morning fight is happening inside that gap.

The turn: the teen body clock runs later

Here is the part many of us never got told.

In the teen years, the body clock shifts. A 2026 paper in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine explains it in plain terms: the hormone that brings sleep comes later at night for teens — by about two hours. So most teens truly struggle to fall asleep early, even when they are tired.

That does not mean a teen who will not get up is doing nothing wrong. It does mean part of this fight is biology, not just attitude. She cannot fall asleep at 10, and she cannot wake up at 6, because her body is running on a clock that moved — and school did not move with it.

One study shows what happens when school does move. In Seattle, high schools pushed the first bell 55 minutes later, from 7:50 to 8:45. Researchers tracked the students and found they slept a median of 34 minutes more a night — from 6 hours 50 minutes up to 7 hours 24 minutes. Grades went up a bit. Tardiness and absences went down at the school that had struggled most with them.

Most of us cannot move the school bell. I know that. But the study still tells a parent something useful: when the night and the morning line up a little better with a teen's real clock, the mornings get easier. So the work we can do at home is on the night side of that line.

The morning fight is won the night before. By 6 a.m., it is mostly too late.

I know this house

I am a dad of six. My kids are 9, 9, 13, 16, 19, and 20. So the school-morning years are not behind me. They are my house right now, and they have been my house for a long time.

What I know about myself is not flattering. When I am stressed, I talk too much. My 13-year-old told me once that our talks turn into disasters because I talk a lot and do not listen. A morning lecture at the bedroom door is me doing that same thing, just louder and earlier. It has never once made a teen move faster. It only makes sure we both start the day angry.

I have a line from my own life that I keep coming back to: guidance is not the same as control. I cannot control a body clock. I cannot drag a near-adult out of bed every day and call that a plan. I can guide the night, agree on the shape of the morning, and then let the results teach what my voice cannot.

In our house the night side of this is not new. For the younger version of the same problem — the phone glowing after lights out — I wrote about what I am trying here: My Kid Keeps Sneaking the Screen at Night. What Actually Works? A teen who is on a screen at midnight is a teen who cannot wake up at six. The two fights are one fight.

But here is the part nobody talks about

When a teen will not get up, most advice makes the parent the alarm clock. Knock. Call. Knock again. Shake. Yell. Drive.

A parent can do that for a week. In a senior year, with college and work and a whole house watching, doing it every day for months breaks something — between the parent and the teen, not just in the morning routine.

So the plan below moves the job, slowly, from the parent's shoulders to the teen's. That part is below, and it is the part worth finishing this article for.

Here is what I am trying

1. Fix the night first, together. Sit down at a calm time — not in the morning — and agree on the night side: screens off and charging outside the bedroom at a set time, lights out at a set time, and the same wake time even on weekends, give or take an hour. The CDC's sleep page for families gives the same shape: set and keep regular bedtimes and wake times, and store phones outside the bedroom at night. I am not saying she will love it. I am saying the talk has to happen once, calmly, instead of every morning at full volume.

2. Make the morning hers. Alarm across the room, not next to the pillow. Clothes and bag ready the night before. Then one calm wake-up from me — one — and after that the morning is hers. If she is late, she is late, and school can say what school says. That part is hard to watch. It is also the only teacher I cannot be at home. For a senior, this matters more, not less: next year there is no parent in the dorm hallway.

3. Keep the door open when it goes wrong. A bad morning gets no lecture at dinner. I ask one question later, when we are both human again: what part of the plan broke — the night, the alarm, or the will? Then we fix that one part and try again. This is not a magic fix. Some days in my house it still fails. I am trying to make the failed days fewer and the angry days rare, and I am still bad at the patient part some mornings.

One clear note: I am a dad, not a doctor. If a teen cannot wake up even after long sleep, sleeps far more than other teens, snores loudly, or her mood drops in a big way, that is a talk for her doctor or the school counselor — not a discipline plan. A body that will not wake can be asking for help, and that question deserves a real answer.

Questions parents ask

Why will my teenager not wake up in the morning?

Often it starts the night before. In the teen years the body clock shifts about two hours later, so sleepiness comes later at night, while school start times stay early. The CDC's 2025 youth data says only 24% of high school students got at least 8 hours of sleep on an average school night, yet teens ages 13 to 18 need 8 to 10 hours. A teen who fell asleep near midnight and must rise at six is being asked to wake in the deepest part of her night.

How do I get my teen up for school without yelling?

Move the work to the night before and the job to the teen. Agree together on a screens-off time, a lights-out time, and a steady wake time. Put the alarm across the room and lay out clothes and the bag at night. In the morning, wake her once, calmly, and let the natural results of a late start land at school rather than starting a daily fight at home. Plan on weeks of steady repeats, and expect some failed days.

Should I keep waking my senior every morning?

Probably not for much longer, and senior year is a reasonable time to hand it over — slowly, with a plan you agree on together, not by going cold one Monday. A senior is months away from a morning no parent can manage for her. Practicing the full routine now, while home is still a safe place to fail at it, is kinder than protecting her from every late start and sending her out unpracticed. If she truly cannot wake despite enough hours in bed, ask her doctor instead of pushing harder.
