A dad asked a question this week that stopped me. He grew up in a culture where children did not do chores. Now he is a father himself, and he does not have a picture in his head for how this part is supposed to work.

His daughter is 6. On her own, she tidies up. Nobody has to ask her. But when he asks her to pick up her toys, it lands badly. Her little sister is 2, and the ask turns into a hard moment in the house.

So he asked plain questions. At what age do you start? What kind of chores? How often? And how do you ask without it going wrong?

If you grew up the same way, you probably know this exact spot. You want your child to help. You just never saw how a parent teaches it.

The numbers: the fight is not the chore. The fight is the asking.

Let us start with how much time this takes in real homes.

A poll of 2,000 parents of children ages 6 to 11, done by OnePoll in June 2026, found that parents spend 38 minutes a day negotiating with their kids. The biggest topics were tidying their rooms, getting ready, and doing homework. That daily back and forth adds up to about 10 full days a year.

Parents in that same poll said they win only 65% of these debates. So about one time in three, the talk ends and the job still is not done.

Read that again slowly. The chore itself takes two minutes. Putting toys in a basket is not hard work for a 6-year-old. The 38 minutes go to the asking, the waiting, and the arguing about the asking. That is why the dad in this question is stuck on the right word. He is not really asking about baskets. He is asking about the talk around the basket.

When do most families start, and what does the research say?

A UK parent survey by Beko found that children typically begin doing chores around ages 7 to 13. The most common job was tidying their own bedroom — nearly two in three children did it on a regular basis. Around 40% helped care for a family pet.

That is what most families do. It is not the same as what the research found when it looked at younger children.

Researchers followed 9,971 children in the United States from kindergarten into third grade. The study was published in the Journal of Developmental and Behavioral Pediatrics in 2019. Children who did chores more often in kindergarten rated themselves higher a few years later. They felt stronger in school work. They felt stronger with other children. They felt better about their own lives.

Children who rarely did chores in kindergarten were more likely to land in the bottom fifth on those same measures in third grade. Doing chores at any level in kindergarten was also linked to better math scores.

I want to say this part with care. That study shows a link. It does not prove that chores alone caused the result. Good homes do many things at once, and no single study can pull them apart. But the direction is hard to ignore, and it points earlier than most families start. The 6-year-old in this question is not too young. She is right in the window that study was looking at.

And what about her 2-year-old sister? A 2-year-old cannot carry a chore list. What she can do is copy. She can carry one toy to the basket while her sister carries the rest. That is not a chore yet. It is her watching her big sister learn that this house is partly hers to care for.

Should kids be paid for chores?

The dad did not ask this in so many words, but it sits under his question. If a chore is work, do we pay for it?

Families split on this, and the Beko survey shows the split in plain numbers. 31% of parents pay an allowance for chores. 17% give no reward at all — in those homes, chores are simply expected. Many other parents use screen time or small treats instead of money.

The same survey found that 62% of parents agree chores help a child build independence. So most of us agree on why chores matter. We just do not agree on the pay part, and there is no single right answer hiding in the data.

Here is a simple way to think about it, and it is only a way of thinking, not a rule. Some jobs belong to living in a house. You made the mess, so you help clean it. Other jobs are extra, above the normal part. A family can pay for the extra kind and still keep the first kind unpaid. The line will look different in every house. What matters is that the child can see the line.

The chore is small. The ask is everything. A child does not learn responsibility from the basket — she learns it from feeling like a real part of the house.

I know this house

I am a dad of six. My kids are 9, 9, 13, 16, 19, and 20. Chores and responsibility have been a red line in our house for a long time. Firm, not soft. That part I never had to learn from a survey.

What I did have to learn is the talk around it. My 13-year-old told me once that our talks turn into disasters because I talk a lot and do not listen. He was right. A chore ask can turn into the same thing — a long speech about one small job, until the job is not the problem anymore. I am.

That is why the agreement my 13-year-old and I are writing together covers chores, right next to the phone. I wrote about that agreement here: Every Night Was a Fight Over the Phone. Here's What We're Trying Instead. It is not signed yet. It is an experiment, and he gets a vote. A rule a child helped build is a different thing from a rule that landed on him from above.

I keep coming back to a line from my own life: guidance is not the same as control. A chore list handed down is control. Sitting with a child, picking one small job together, and showing her how it is done — that is guidance. The basket gets filled either way. Only one of those ways teaches her anything.

But here is the part nobody talks about

Most chore talk is about a clean house. Parents want help. The floor is a mess. The day is long.

But the clean house is the small part. The big part is what a child decides about herself while she works. Am I a person who helps in this house, or a person things are done for? She will answer that question young, from small moments, long before she can say it in words.

So how you start matters more than how much she does. That part is below, and it is the part worth finishing this article for.

Here is what I am trying

1. Start with one job, and make it hers. Not a list. One job that belongs to her every day. For a 6-year-old, that can be as small as her own toys going into her own basket before dinner. Same job, same time, every day. When it is always hers, the daily asking shrinks. The routine asks for you. This is not a magic fix. Some days the basket still sits there, and I still have to ask. I am still bad at keeping the ask short.

2. Ask once, in plain words, and get down at her level. I am learning to say the whole ask in one short line. Not a speech. Not three reminders before I even finish the first one. "Toys in the basket, then dinner." Then I wait. The waiting is the hard part for me. My kids have heard my long version many times. The short version is the one I am practicing now.

3. Let the little one copy, with a tiny part of the same job. The 2-year-old gets one toy to carry. That is all. She will drop it some days. She will carry it the wrong way on others. It does not matter. She is learning the shape of helping before she is old enough for a real chore, and her big sister gets to be the one she copies. One day the copy becomes the habit.

One clear note: I am a dad, not a child expert. If a child is struggling in a bigger way — not just refusing a job, but struggling with daily tasks other children her age handle with ease — that is a talk for her doctor, not for a blog post. Chores are a family question. That other question deserves a real answer from someone who can see your child.

Questions parents ask

At what age should kids start doing chores?

Many families start around ages 7 to 13, according to a Beko UK parent survey, but research points earlier: in a study of 9,971 U.S. children, doing chores in kindergarten was linked to higher self-competence in third grade. A simple starting point is age 5 or 6 with one small daily job, like putting her own toys in one basket, and a 2- or 3-year-old can copy with a tiny part of the same job.

How do I ask my child to do a chore without a fight?

Keep the ask short, plain, and the same every day. OnePoll found in June 2026 that parents of kids ages 6 to 11 spend 38 minutes a day negotiating about tidying, getting ready, and homework, and win only 65% of those debates. One fixed job at one fixed time cuts the daily talk down, because the routine does the asking instead of a new speech each night.

Should I pay my child for doing chores?

Families split on this: a Beko UK survey found 31% of parents pay an allowance for chores, while 17% give no reward and treat chores as simply expected. One common middle path is to keep basic jobs — cleaning up after herself, helping with her own space — unpaid as part of living in the house, and pay only for extra jobs above that line.
