A parent asked a question this week that I have asked in my own head many times. Her son is 6. The baby was being put to sleep, and he took a loud toy right to the door.

He knows the rule. He has heard it many times. So her question was simple: when do they start thinking before they do something?

If you have a young child, you probably know this exact moment. The child is not being bad. The child is not even being sneaky. He just… did it. And you are standing there tired, wondering if the lesson will ever land.

The numbers: we expect the pause years too early

Let us start with us, the parents, because the research does.

A large parent survey by ZERO TO THREE found what it called an expectation gap: about half of parents believe children can control themselves much earlier than they actually can. Not a few of us. About one in two.

That one number explains a lot of hard evenings. If I believe my child should already be able to stop and think, then every loud toy at the wrong door feels like defiance. If he cannot do it yet — if the skill is still being built — then the same moment is just a young brain doing what young brains do.

Same child. Same toy. Two very different evenings, depending on which story I believe.

The turn: knowing and pausing are two different skills

Here is the part that changed how I hear that parent's question.

Knowing a rule lives in one place. Pausing before you act lives in another. A 6-year-old can repeat your rule back to you word for word and still grab the toy, because the part of the brain that stops the hand is not the part that stored the words.

Researchers can watch that pausing system still being built. In a study published this year in Communications Psychology, 113 children ages 3 to 6 played a card game where the rules changed in the middle. Brain imaging showed the older children doing better, with the front part of the brain — the part that handles flexible thinking and self-control — working in a more focused way. At 6, that system is still becoming organized. It is not finished. It is not even close to finished.

And the timeline is longer than most of us were ever told. Impulse control only begins to develop at about age 3 and a half to 4. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child puts it plainly: children are not born with these skills, and the full range of them keeps growing through the teen years and into early adulthood.

So when that parent asks, "When do they start thinking before doing?" the honest answer is: he has already started. He started two or three years ago. It is just that starting and finishing are about twenty years apart, and nobody hands parents that timeline at the hospital.

He is not ignoring the rule. His brakes are still being built — and a lecture cannot install brakes.

I know this house

I am a dad of six. My kids are 9, 9, 13, 16, 19, and 20. That means the age-6 stage has passed through my house six times. I wish I could tell you I was patient all six times. I was not.

What I know about myself is not flattering. When I am tired, I explain. And then I explain the explanation. My 13-year-old told me once that our talks turn into disasters because I talk a lot and do not listen. A long speech to a 6-year-old about thinking first is the same habit in a smaller room. The words feel like teaching. To him, they are mostly weather.

I keep coming back to a line from my own life: guidance is not the same as control. I cannot control the speed his brain grows. I can guide the room around him — where the loud toys live, what happens in the same calm way every time — and let a hundred small repeats do what one big speech cannot.

And to be fair to the kids, we adults are not finished either. I wrote about the grown-up version of this same problem — a parent trying to pause before his voice goes up — here: How Do I Get My Husband to Stop Raising His Voice at Our Son? We ask a 6-year-old to stop and think. Then we lose our own pause by dinner. The building work runs both ways in a house.

But here is the part nobody talks about

Most advice for this problem tells the parent to explain the rule better, or louder, or one more time.

But if the gap is in the pausing, not in the knowing, then more explaining is more of the thing that already is not working. What a young child can actually use is a rule so small it fits in one breath, a room set up so the rule is easier to keep, and the same calm response every single time — so his body learns the pattern while his brain catches up.

Here is what I am trying

1. Make the rule small, and tie it to a place. "Be quiet" is big and fuzzy. "Loud toys stay in the playroom" is small, and a 6-year-old can hold it. One rule, one place, said the same way each time. I am not trying to teach him to think about the whole house. I am trying to give his still-growing pause one simple job.

2. Walk it back calmly, every time, instead of giving the speech. When the loud toy travels, I go get it — or walk with him to take it back — with as few words as I can manage. No lecture about the baby. No "how many times have I told you." The same quiet trip, every time, like a song he has heard so often his body knows it. This is not a magic fix. It is slow. Some evenings it fails three times before bed. I am still bad at keeping my own voice low on the third trip.

3. Practice the pause when nothing is wrong. Freeze games. Red light, green light. The silly games where you dance and stop, dance and stop. At calm times, stopping is fun, and the body gets practice at the exact move bedtime will ask for. I am not claiming games fix anything. I am saying practice at a happy time costs nothing, and it beats only ever practicing in a fight.

One clear note: I am a dad, not a doctor. Every child acts before thinking sometimes — that is childhood, not a diagnosis. But if a child's impulsivity is far beyond other kids his age, if someone gets hurt, or if his teacher is raising the same worry, that is a talk for his pediatrician, not a blog post. Asking that question early is a strength, not a failure.

Questions parents ask

At what age do children start thinking before they act?

Impulse control only begins to develop at about age 3 and a half to 4, and it grows slowly from there. A 2026 study of 113 children ages 3 to 6 found older children were better at a rule-switching task, with the self-control part of the brain still becoming more focused at age 6. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child says these skills keep maturing through the teen years into early adulthood, so a 6-year-old who acts first and thinks later is developing on schedule, not misbehaving on purpose.

Why does my child know the rule but still break it?

Because knowing a rule and pausing before an action are two different skills, run by different parts of development. A young child can often repeat a rule perfectly and still grab the loud toy, because the "brakes" system is years behind the memory system. About half of parents in the ZERO TO THREE Tuning In survey believed children could control themselves much earlier than they actually can, which is why this moment surprises so many of us.

How can I help my 6-year-old learn to stop and think?

Make the rule small and tied to a place, like "loud toys stay in the playroom," and respond the same calm way every time it is broken — walk the toy back with few words instead of a lecture. Practice pausing during play with freeze games and red light, green light, so the skill grows when everyone is calm. Expect months of repeats: you are training a skill that is still under construction, and steady repeats are how it gets built.
